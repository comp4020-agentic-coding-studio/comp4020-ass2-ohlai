Teaching resumes after the break, and the course turns. Everything before now
was about one person and a duration. From here the question is who else is
waiting and on what terms.

Larson argues that what infuriates people in queues is often not the delay
but the violation of order, and that the outcome of a queueing experience
varies with delay in a way that makes average time in queue a poor measure of
anything. Rafaeli, Barron and Haber then put people in queues under
controlled conditions and found that a multiple queue structure produced a
sense of injustice even where no objective inequality existed.

Read that last finding carefully, because it is the strongest claim in this
half of the course. The structure produced the feeling on its own. Nobody had
to actually be treated unfairly.

## In the workshop

Find a queue in the wild and record its discipline rather than its length.
Who goes next, how is that decided, and how does a person standing in it
know? Then find the priority lane, because there is almost always one, and
say what buys a place in it.

## But wait, there's more

A deli ticket dispenser makes your position in the queue into a physical
object you can hold. It also lets you stop standing in line, which is the
part that gets less attention, because it converts a queue into a set of
people sitting down. Take a ticket somewhere this week and notice whether you
still watch the counter.
