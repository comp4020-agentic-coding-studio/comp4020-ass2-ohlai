The Intervention brief lands in this half of the course for a reason, and
this is the week that makes the reason explicit.

Schwartz established in 1975 that waiting tracks power. Who waits for whom is
a statement about relative standing, and it holds across settings that have
nothing else in common. Auyero then took the argument into welfare offices,
registration queues and a polluted shantytown, and found the waiting doing
work on the people inside it. What is being taught in those rooms is
patience, and patience here means the absence of any other option.

If week 5 asked what an illusion costs the person it is shown to, this week
asks the harder version. Who is that person, and why is it so consistently
the same people.

## In the workshop

Take one service and map its waits by user group. Who gets the priority lane,
who gets the phone queue, who gets the room with chairs. Then say what would
have to change for the distribution to be different, and what that change
would cost, and to whom.

## But wait, there's more

A now serving display turns a queue into a broadcast. Everyone in the room
can see how fast the number is moving, which means everyone can also see the
moment it stops. It is the rare piece of queue infrastructure that gives
information to the people waiting rather than about them.
