Everything you have measured this semester was the experienced wait. This
week introduces the other one, and the two do not agree.

Redelmeier and Kahneman recorded pain in real time through colonoscopy and
lithotripsy and compared it with what patients said afterwards. Remembered
discomfort tracked the peak and the last few minutes. Procedure length
barely showed up. The 1993 cold water study makes the same point more
sharply: people offered a repeat chose the longer trial, which was 60 seconds
of cold plus 30 seconds of slightly less cold, over the shorter one.

The design consequence is immediate and slightly unpleasant. You can improve
a remembered wait by extending it, provided the extension is the gentlest
part. Decide this week whether you are willing to do that.

This is also the week to read the marks policy, which holds results until
week 12. That delay is designed, it is ours, and it is on the syllabus so
that you can argue with it while the reading is in front of you.

## In the workshop

Take a wait you have already documented and change only its final ten per
cent. Same total duration, same information, different ending. Then say what
you would have to measure to know whether it worked, and which of those
measurements the person waiting would consent to.

## But wait, there's more

The prompt that asks you to stay on the line for a short survey arrives at
the one moment the peak-end rule says matters most. It is placed after the
resolution and before the hang up, which is the last thing you will remember
about the call. Whether that placement is a measurement or an intervention
is not obvious, and that is the interesting part.
